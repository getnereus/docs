    State of AI in Platform Engineering Vol. 2¶

    • Source: State of AI in Platform Engineering, Volume 2 (https://weaveintelligence.io/research/state-of-ai-in-platform-engineering-2026) (Weave Intelligence, 2026; authors Sam Barlien, Luca Galante, Florian Lipp; partners Thoughtworks, Vultr, Syntasso, env zero, Microsoft). Methodology (p. 55): 350 global practitioners, data collected from April to August 2026, 24 questions; profile: individual contributors 31%, team leads 27%, heads of function 19%, VP/director/C-level 23%. The maturity levels and the gains are self-reported (p. 18, p. 21). Page numbering below = the PDF's printed numbering.
    • Checked on 2026-10-29: the links open.

    Summary¶

    • The report's central finding: 38% of organizations ship at least twice as much since adopting AI, but only 8% describe the return as transformative. The whole report explores this gap (p. 8, p. 54).
    • The bottleneck is not the model, it is the platform: "platform readiness" leads the obstacles to scaling AI (27%), "model capability" is fourth (11%) (p. 24).
    • Maturity pays off in steps, not in a gradient: adding agents without rebuilding the platform adds 6 percentage points of throughput; rebuilding the platform adds 24 (p. 21).
    • Code generation has industrialized, absorption has not: 35% still validate AI-generated changes by human reading alone and only 17% run automated validation loops (p. 26).
    • Governance is the weakest point: a quarter run agents with borrowed human credentials, 12% with a shared service account and 18% do not know how agent identity is managed (p. 38). The Agentic Engineering Platform (AEP) is presented as the evolution of the IDP, not its replacement (p. 28-30).

    • "Nobody is waiting for a better model." (p. 7; reinforced on p. 54)
    • "Generation industrialized. Absorption did not." (p. 7)
    • "An agent that cannot be identified cannot be audited, scoped, or switched off without switching off a person" (p. 7)
    • "tighter governance actually raises the autonomy ceiling instead of lowering it." (p. 41)
    • "Organizations that throw agents at a Level 1 substrate will find their agents moving faster than their guardrails." (p. 30)

    Concepts and frameworks¶

    • Four levels of agentic development (p. 18): Level 0 Human is the loop (no agents; the IDP baseline), Level 1 Human in the loop (AI suggests, human executes), Level 2 Human on the loop (agents generate PRs in parallel), Level 3 Humans as orchestrators (agents work continuously from observed signals, review by exception), Level 4 Fully autonomous (systems of agents inside guardrails designed by humans).
    • Agentic Engineering Platform (AEP) (p. 27-30, glossary p. 56): what the IDP becomes when agents are its users; an evolution of the IDP, not a replacement.
    • Three layers of the AEP (p. 29): tooling layer (the five planes: developer control, integration and delivery, resource, security, observability; marked as the IDP), paths layer (repeatable, governed, machine-executable routes) and agent infrastructure layer (identity, context, capability, execution, evaluation, security, observability, all as code).
    • Path types (p. 29): Deterministic (pipeline, same output for the same input), Probabilistic (agent, "Evals become the test suite"), Hybrid (probabilistic generation with deterministic gates in a loop). Recommendation: name the type of each path and require deterministic gates on irreversible effects.
    • The four prerequisites of Level 3 (p. 10, p. 50): dispatch path, validation loop, scoped identity, sandboxed environments, which only pay off together.
    • Validation gate vs. validation loop (p. 30, glossary p. 58): a gate is a binary checkpoint; a loop returns the failure to the agent with the reason until the deterministic checks pass ("first-pass failure is convergence, not defect").
    • Six return vectors (p. 34): delivery velocity, outcome predictability, operational resiliency, maintenance overhead, organizational learning, structural incentives.
    • Metric "cost per accepted output" instead of cost per token (p. 36, p. 51); four functions of token FinOps: attribute every call, watch spend velocity, enforce hard limits in the platform, produce an audit trail (p. 36).
    • Six coverage questions (p. 50): is there a governed, machine-executable path in Plan, Code, Review, Test, Deploy, Monitor? 0-2 yes = the substrate is the work; 3-4 = "expensive middle"; 5-6 = the constraint has already moved to cost and architecture.
    • Five recommendations (p. 50-51): build the four Level 3 prerequisites together; close the validation loop before adding agent capability; fix attribution (no borrowed human credentials); gate by consequence, not by category (possible damage and speed of detection); measure value, not output.
    • Governance patterns of the leaders (p. 41): policy, identity and network inherited from the workspace; human/agent privilege separation (least privilege, writes only through pull request); ephemeral workspaces; controlled, recorded failure instead of a silent incident.
    • AI implementation plateau (p. 12, glossary p. 56) and "Platform as a product, golden paths, standardization" as differentiators of those who got past the plateau (p. 12).
    • Thinking in Platforms and the "Paths to Outcome" model (p. 47); IDP defined in the glossary as a platform that standardizes paths, enables self-service and reduces cognitive load (p. 57).

    Relevance by plane¶

    • Developer Control Plane: agents are already platform customers in 29% of organizations and 43% expect the role to shift to governance and guardrails (rows "Platform customers" and "Expectation of the role"); the dispatch path as the first new path of Level 2 (p. 56); maturity in steps (the throughput-by-level rows and 6 vs. 24 p.p.).
    • Integration & Delivery Plane: absorption bottleneck (35% human reading only vs. 17% loops; review bandwidth from 12% to 23% from Level 1 to 2); validation loop as the engine (p. 30); AI code does not degrade stability (39% improves, 12% worsens); Plan/Code/Review/Test/Deploy/Monitor coverage (p. 50).
    • Resource Plane: 65% use AI to generate IaC and 66% for error and log analysis (p. 15, p. 39); current controls inspect code, not the effect on the provisioned environment; policy-as-code and sandboxed execution are still a minority (p. 39); gate by consequence (p. 51).
    • Observability Plane: token FinOps (13% do not track, 14% do not know, 15% in Levels 1 and 2; attribution by team, path and model; "cost per accepted output"); shadow AI (47% minimal, 27% significant, 8% no visibility; "observability has to precede restriction", p. 41); 66% use AI in log analysis.
    • Security Plane: agent identity (25% human credentials, 12% service account, 18% do not know, 19% dynamic by policy); 37% govern by policy vs. 13% block; risks (64% data exposure, 52% hallucinations, 47% unmanaged use, 43% control bypass, 37% prompt injection); leaders' patterns (workspace-level, least privilege, ephemeral workspaces, p. 41).
    • Portal/catalog: the report does not address portals directly (no mention of Backstage or a catalog in the pages read); use only the concept of a path as a "repeatable route from intent to usable output" (p. 29), golden paths as a differentiator (p. 12) and the expansion of customers (business users 48%, data/ML 49%, p. 43) to justify a single portal for humans and agents. Kratix appears only as a product of the partner Syntasso (p. 5-6), not as a research finding.

    • Agents/AI: maturity (41/31/14/4/9%), throughput by level (30/36/60/79%), 6 vs. 24 p.p., the four Level 3 prerequisites, path types, tooling vs. agent infrastructure, the forecast of more than 30% at Level 3 or above, investment priorities (31% SDLC automation); why "platform readiness" (27%) beats "model capability" (11%).

    • Definition of the levels (Figure 5, p. 18): Level 0 "Human is the loop" (no agents; the IDP baseline); Level 1 "Human in the loop" (AI suggests, humans execute); Level 2 "Human on the loop" (agents generate PRs in parallel; humans trigger and verify behavior); Level 3 "Humans as orchestrators" (agents work continuously from observed signals; review by exception); Level 4 "Fully autonomous" (systems of agents start and finish work inside guardrails designed by humans).
